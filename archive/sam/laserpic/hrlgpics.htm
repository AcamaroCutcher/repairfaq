    Photos of Honeywell Helium-Neon Ring Laser Gyros

    Note: Most links open in a single new tab or window for the individual photos or Web Albums depending on your browser's settings.

    These are photos of Honeywell helium-neon laser-based ring laser gyros GG1342, GG1320AN, GG1320AF, and GG1308s in the H1700AG13 and H1700AG59 IMUs. This is already believed to be the most comprehensive selection of detailed publically accessible RLG photos on the Web - or in the explored Universe. ;-) That is, until the Kearfott IRU/RLG photos were posted. ;-) While there are tantilizing references to a few other Honewell RLGs, they are so much less common that finding samples - if they even exist - is likely to be orders or magnitude less likely than buying viable dinosaur eggs on eBay. ;-)

    Much more information on ring laser gyros and details on all of these may be found at Sam's Laser FAQ Chapter: Laser Intruments and Applications in the sections starting with "Ring Laser Gyros".

    • Honeywell GG1342 Ring Laser Gyro:

      These are photos of several different samples of complete Honeywell GG1342 ring laser gyros and related components. The GG1342 acquired first already had had its skins completely removed. That must have hurt! ;( ;-) It was used for all the Gallery views and the majority of the Web Album interior views. Its PART NO and manufacturing date were lost with the skins, but based on date codes found on several parts, it is from the early 1990s. The first 4 exterior views are of a unit manufactured in 1985 with PART NO GG1342AE07. The next 4 exterior views and many of the powered views are of a unit manufactured in 2013 with PART NO GG1342AE44. And the GG1342s in the Inertial Platform Web in the Album have PART NO GG1342AE03 with a manufacturing date of 1994. However, the internal construction of all of these GG1342s is virtually identical even though they span 28 years.

      Also included are photos of the HeNe laser power supply PCB with individual outputs for the three GG1342s in the inertial platform. (The first Web Album photo.) And an acceleromater because it would feel lonely if left out. ;-)

      The GG1342 was the first RLG Honeywell produced commercially, but is structurally and functionally similar to later models like the still current GG1320AN (below). Unlike the GG1320 and HG1700, all known versions of the GG1342 are believed to be very similar.

    • Honeywell GG1320 Ring Laser Gyro:

      The Honeywell GG1320 RLG is used in numerous inertial navigation systems and the current version is still considered state-of-the-art. It has been in production in some form at least back to the mid-1990s. And as of 2026 and Honeywell claims that over 500,000 units have been delivered.

      There are two known versions of the GG1320, the GG1320AN and GG1320AF, though each may have multiple revisions. Here is a montage which also includes one only found in a photo from the Honeywell Website: Known Variations of Honeywell GG1320 Ring Laser Gyro. The GG1320AN and GG1320AF are covered below; nothing is known about the one in the Honeywell photo except that is probably an older (and possibly the original) digital implementation.

      • GG1320AN: This is the model found on the Honeywell GG1320AN Digital Ring Laser Gyro Web Page and the only one returned via a Web search. (That is, until the information here on the GG1320AF had been uploaded!) The GG1320AN in the gallery is from 2001.

        Honeywell calls it a "Digital Ring Laser Gyro" as all the control electronics are contained within the 3.45 inch diameter, 1 pound package. Input is +5 VDC and +15 VDC, a Reset pulse, and Sample Request pulses. The status and angle data are returned via RS422. This has been confirmed by testing a GG1320AN. ;-)

        There used to be a color photo of a version of the GG1320AN open showing the PCBs (but NOT the Laser Block) on the Honeywell Website though it differed from the one documented here and may be an earlier revision. This is a copy: Honeywell GG1320AN Photo. I'm hoping to find samples of that one. And it has been superseeded by a much more boring photo of the outside of the GG1320AN case. ;-(

      • GG1320AF: This is most likely an older and probably the original version. All known samples have dates prior to around the year 2000. The only on-line reference with this part number was from the paper: "Qualification of the International Space Station rate gyro assembly, AIAA-98-4326", AIAA Guidance, Navigation, and Control Conference and Exhibit, 10-12 August 1998, Boston, MA. So it may have been special for the ISS. ;-) While believed to have functional specifications similar to those of the GG1320AN, the GG1320AF interface connector has external separate pins for the HeNe laser high voltage whereas the GG1320AN has an internal HV power supply that runs on 15 VDC. In fact, the GG1320AF shares much of its DNA with the GG1342: There is no internal microprocessor, only basic analog ICs, discrete transistors, and passives. As such it is NOT a digital RLG!

      The GG1320AN shown below is believed to be from 2001 based on the label and date codes found on some ICs. The complete designation on the label is: PART NO GG1320AN13 SERIES 39.

    • Honeywell HG1700 IMU with Triple GG1308 Ring Laser Gyro:

      The Honeywell HG1700 IMG (Inertial Measurement Unit) is used in a variety of applications like missiles, UAVs, UUVs, and and other drones where the lower performance and life expectancy of the very small GG1308 RLGs (leg length of 0.8 inches) is adequate.

      The HG1700 consists of three GG1308 RLGs, three vibrating beam quartz accelerometers, and the integrated power supplies and communications and control PCB, all in a package under 4x4x4 inches overall weighing less than 2 pounds.

      The first few photos are similar to those of the HG1700 found on the Web; many of the others are of various stages of disassembly and its guts, and are not available elsewhere anywhere else. :-)

      Since I've yet to find specs on the external connector to be able to power the entire HG1700, the photos of the RLGs lit in all their glory were taken using an adjustable output HeNe laser power supply with an external 3.3M ohm ballast resistor for each of the three anodes and six cathodes. The power supply voltage is around 1.5 kV for all the photos which includes the voltage drop across the external ballasts, internal ballasts and other circuitry which is part of the potted VMI module, and the discharges themselves. The starting voltage is between 1.5 and 2.0 kV. The operating current is under 0.105 mA for each discharge with a discharge voltage of around 0.5 kV. The dropout current is under 0.067 mA at a total operating voltage less than 1 kV. Note that much of the total voltage is made up of the drops across the external ballasts, which would not be present for actual operation, though there is probably some amount of active current regulation for at least half the discharge paths to fine tune lasing gain. So the high voltage inside the HG1700 under normal conditions isn't that high. :)

      If anyone has access to documentation, please contact me via the links at the top or bottom of this page.

      This one has a manufacturing data of 2004. The case at least is very slightly different than the one above and more difficult to get inside. For the HG1700AG59 it was simply a matter of slicing the resilient mount and lock ring. Then the top and bottom could be separated without any additional trauma. For the HG1700AG13, heat gun was used to loosen the thread locker securing the resilient mount so it could be unscrewed. But the top and bottom of the case are apparently press-fit or fused somehow because no reasonable amount of force or heat allowed them to come apart. So a hacksaw was used to slice the case around its circumference approximately halfway between the bottom and the rib. Luckily, the cut was just at the edge of the triple GG1308 mount ring. It, along with the triple accelerometers, could then be unplugged from the PCB above it and removed intact. Since some HG1700s are considered "single use" in missiles and such, there would be no need to be able to disassemble the IMU, But apparently, Honeywell thought better of in the later HG1700AG59 so it could be disassembled non-destructively. Or simply that it's intended application might be longer lived than a missile. ;-) The RLG assembly appears to be the same except perhaps for the part number on the potted VMI module, but the PCB reflect earlier technology with less integration. The accelerometers are the same.
